Focus Skill Targeted: Audience-Centered Rhetorical Adaptation and Counterargument Preemption Through Global Revision

When I entered ENC 2135 at Florida State University this fall, my understanding of academic writing was largely mechanical. Coming from a background focused on business mathematics, technical systems, and Management Information Systems (MIS), I viewed composition much like configuring a network router or writing a database query: follow a standardized structural template, plug in verifiable facts, and let the data speak for itself. I assumed that if an argument was logically valid and supported by statistics, any reasonable reader would be persuaded regardless of how the text was framed. However, completing Project 1, Project 2, and the global revision process for this electronic portfolio dismantled that assumption. Across this semester, the single most transformative concept I learned is audience-centered rhetorical adaptation, specifically the skill of diagnosing an institutional audience's hidden anxieties and preempting their counterarguments through structural and tonal design. Rather than treating genres as static formatting checklists, I learned to treat genres as strategic social responses tailored to the values, constraints, and lived realities of specific discourse communities.

My initial awakening to the power of discourse communities and rhetorical boundaries began during Project 1, my investigative field essay examining artificial intelligence integration within clinical healthcare workflows. When I first began researching AI in medicine, I planned to write a broad endorsement of AI-driven patient triage and automated diagnostic translation tools. However, as I compiled my annotated bibliography and analyzed peer-reviewed clinical literature, I noticed a sharp divide between how software engineers and medical practitioners evaluate technology. While developers prioritize algorithmic speed, healthcare professionals prioritize liability mitigation, diagnostic accuracy, and patient safety. Recognizing these community values forced me to pivot my Project 1 thesis: instead of advocating for high-risk automated patient triage (which suffers from hallucination risks and algorithmic bias), I argued that ambient clinical documentation and automated Electronic Health Record (EHR) notetaking represent the safest, highest-value application of healthcare AI. Project 1 taught me that credible writing within a professional field requires adopting that community's risk-averse ethos rather than imposing an outsider's enthusiasm.

While Project 1 introduced me to analyzing a single professional discourse community, Project 2, the Multigenre Persuasive Campaign, tested my ability to adapt a highly controversial policy argument across wildly different audiences. For Project 2, I advocated for repealing Florida's statutory "Gun-Free Zone" mandate on public university campuses (Florida Statute § 790.06(12)(a)) and replacing it with a vetted "Enhanced Campus Carry" (ECC) framework at Florida State University for Concealed Weapon License (CWL) holders aged 21 and older. Because few topics generate more immediate emotional friction on a college campus, I could not rely on one uniform message. I had to construct three separate genres: a Legislative Policy and Criminology Brief directed at the Florida House Judiciary Committee, the FSU Board of Trustees, and FSUPD Command Staff; an Operational Safety and Compliance Handout aimed at 21-and-older student CWL holders and student veterans; and a campus newspaper op-ed in the FSView & Florida Flambeau targeting female students and night-class commuters. Moving between these three genres taught me how affordances and constraints shape persuasion. What worked as compelling spatial pathos in the student op-ed, such as describing the dark midnight walk from Strozier Library to the Woodward Avenue parking garage after waiting 45 minutes for a SAFE Connection van, had to be completely stripped out of the legislative policy brief and replaced with longitudinal crime audits from Texas Senate Bill 11.

Although my final draft of Project 2 succeeded in separating these voices, selecting Genre 1 (the Legislative Policy and Criminology Brief) for global revision in this ePortfolio revealed how much deeper my audience adaptation needed to go. As Chapter 3 of The Bedford Handbook explains, local revision merely polishes sentence-level wording and grammar, whereas global revision requires re-evaluating the overarching architecture of a text: its purpose, thesis scope, organizational logic, and evidentiary depth. When I revisited my Project 2 Policy Brief with fresh eyes, I realized that while my original brief effectively proved why disarmed students are vulnerable during active-shooter events like the November 2014 Strozier Library shooting and the April 2025 FSU Student Union shooting, its organizational structure was still one-sided. It read like an external advocacy group lecturing university administrators rather than an internal risk-management blueprint written by a policy partner who genuinely understood administrative constraints.

To execute a true global revision of Genre 1, I made three major structural and substantive overhauls that are visible in the revised portfolio composition. First, I narrowed and sharpened the thesis within the Executive Summary from a broad statutory exception into a concrete, multi-tiered administrative pilot program: integrating the ECC endorsement directly into FSU's existing biometric Seminole Card database so FSUPD dispatchers have real-time verification of authorized carriers. Second, I reorganized the body of the brief by adding an entirely new section titled Institutional Feasibility, Dormitory Infrastructure, and Liability Mitigation. In my original Project 2 Rationale, I had admitted that requiring UL-rated biometric safes in shared dorms created a financial barrier for lower-income students and left lingering liability questions for university housing. Rather than leaving that flaw unaddressed, my global revision solves it directly inside the policy brief: it proposes reallocating a portion of applicant certification fees into a campus safe-subsidy program and cites specific sovereign-immunity liability protections modeled after Texas SB 11, which shields universities from civil suits arising from the actions of state-licensed permit holders. Third, I expanded the evidentiary base by adding an explicit Inter-Agency Tactical Dispatch Protocol section that addresses FSUPD's biggest operational fear, friendly-fire confusion during an active lockdown, by proposing encrypted blue-force notification alerts through the Seminole SAFE app.

The two supporting artifacts included in this portfolio further illustrate how my composing practices evolved from linear drafting into structured rhetorical planning. My first artifact, the Audience & Genre Rhetorical Matrix created during the planning phase of Project 2, demonstrates how I now map out audience resistances, institutional values, genre affordances, and constraints before writing a single paragraph of prose. Looking back at this matrix shows how intentionally I separated the empirical logos required by the Florida Legislature from the imperative command tone required for student permit holders. My second artifact, my Global vs. Local Revision Diagnostic Audit, captures the exact analytical method I used to critique my own Project 2 draft. By forcing myself to identify structural blind spots, such as failing to address university insurance premiums and campus police dispatch logistics, before touching sentence-level mechanics, I transformed revision from a last-minute proofreading chore into a strategic redesign of the argument.

Ultimately, the rhetorical skills I honed in ENC 2135 and refined through this global revision will directly shape how I communicate in my future academic coursework and professional career in Management Information Systems. In the MIS field, technical specialists constantly have to persuade two vastly different audiences that mirror the exact divide I navigated in Project 2: C-suite executives who care about return on investment, cybersecurity liability, and regulatory compliance, and everyday end-users who care about workflow convenience and practical usability. Whether I am writing an executive proposal to migrate a regional enterprise off a legacy ERP database, drafting an acceptable-use cybersecurity policy for non-technical employees, or presenting a data-governance audit in upper-division business courses like GEB 3213, I will never again assume that raw data speaks for itself. Instead, I will begin every communication task by diagnosing the rhetorical situation, choosing the genre conventions that match my audience's discourse habits, and using global revision to anticipate and resolve my reader's deepest objections before they even ask.

Legislative Policy & Criminology Brief: Establishing the FSU Enhanced Campus Carry (ECC) Pilot Framework

MEMORANDUM
TO: Florida House Judiciary Committee, Florida State University Board of Trustees, and FSU Police Department (FSUPD) Command Staff
FROM: Collegiate Coalition for Constitutional and Campus Safety Reform at Florida State University
DATE: 16 November 2026
SUBJECT: Statutory and Administrative Blueprint: Amending F.S. § 790.06(12)(a) to Implement a Biometrically Verified "Enhanced Campus Carry" (ECC) Framework

1. Executive Summary and Revised Statutory Thesis

Under Florida Statute § 790.06(12)(a), Florida State University currently operates as a mandated "Gun-Free Zone," prohibiting vetted, fingerprint-checked Concealed Weapon License (CWL) holders aged 21 and older from carrying defensive firearms on campus property (Florida Legislature). While intended to deter violence, statutory prohibitions on an open urban campus without perimeter metal detectors or armed security checkpoints do not prevent malicious actors from bringing weapons onto campus grounds; they exclusively disarm law-abiding citizens. Rather than proposing unrestricted permitless carry on university grounds, this brief outlines a two-pronged legislative and administrative solution: amending F.S. § 790.06(12)(a) to authorize a tiered Enhanced Campus Carry (ECC) endorsement for CWL holders aged 21 and older (specifically student veterans, faculty, and upper-division commuters), while integrating ECC credentialing directly into FSU's encrypted Seminole Card and FSUPD computer-aided dispatch (CAD) infrastructure.

2. Empirical and Case Study Analysis: The Fatal Response-Time Gap at FSU

Opponents of campus carry frequently project that allowing licensed adults to carry concealed firearms in academic spaces will trigger classroom disputes or negligent discharges. However, both FSU's institutional history and multi-state criminological audits demonstrate that statutory bans merely guarantee a defenseless victim pool during the critical window before police breach a scene:

A. The November 2014 Strozier Library Shooting: When an armed perpetrator opened fire inside Strozier Library during late-night study hours, three students were shot before FSUPD and Tallahassee Police neutralized the threat (Florida State University Police Department 4). Trapped inside the library was Nathan Scott, a 21-year-old student CWL holder and founder of a student firearms safety organization, who had been legally forced by F.S. § 790.06 to lock his licensed handgun inside his parked vehicle prior to entering the building, leaving him legally barred from defending himself or his peers.

B. The April 2025 FSU Student Union Shooting: Over a decade later, an armed assailant targeted high-traffic campus zones near the Student Union, exploiting the certainty that students inside the core of campus were disarmed. Even with FSUPD's rapid response, casualties occurred inside the initial 180-second window before perimeter officers could enter the perimeter.

C. Longitudinal Data from Peer Postsecondary Systems: Twelve states, including Utah (since 2004), Colorado (since 2012), and Texas (since 2016 via Senate Bill 11), allow concealed carry on public university campuses. Multi-year crime audits conducted across the University of Texas and Texas A&M systems following SB 11 revealed zero statistically significant increases in aggravated assaults, negligent discharges, or firearm-related suicides in academic buildings (Texas Department of Public Safety 12). Furthermore, data from the Crime Prevention Research Center confirms that CWL holders are convicted of felony offenses at less than one-sixth the rate of the general public (Lott 8).

3. Institutional Feasibility, Liability Immunity, and Dormitory Infrastructure

A primary objection raised by university general counsel and risk-management officers is that permitting firearms on campus could expose Florida State University to civil tort liability or inflate institutional insurance premiums. To neutralize this risk, the proposed statutory amendment mirrors Section 411.208 of the Texas Government Code (enacted under SB 11), which grants explicit statutory immunity to public universities, boards of trustees, and campus police officers from civil damages resulting from the actions of an authorized ECC license holder.

Furthermore, to prevent unattended vehicle thefts in perimeter commuter garages (such as Woodward, Traditions, and Call Street) without creating socioeconomic barriers for student permit holders residing on campus, the ECC framework establishes a self-funding fee architecture. Each applicant pays a $125 ECC certification fee to FDLE and FSUPD: $85 funds the mandatory 8-hour FSUPD tactical qualification course, while $40 is deposited into a dedicated Campus Safe-Storage Grant Fund that provides free, university-approved UL-rated biometric lockboxes to verified student veterans and 21+ upperclassmen residing in on-campus apartments such as Ragans or Traditions Hall.

4. Inter-Agency FSUPD Dispatch Integration and Sensitive-Area Carve-Outs

To address FSUPD command staff concerns regarding target identification during active-threat lockdowns, the ECC framework pairs strict sensitive-area exclusions with digital dispatch integration:

A. Seminole Card & Seminole SAFE Blue-Force Integration: Upon passing the 8-hour live-fire accuracy, retention, and "shoot/don't-shoot" qualification, a permit holder's ECC status is linked to their FSUID. During an active "FSU ALERT" lockdown, ECC holders who shelter in place inside a classroom can tap a one-touch "Authorized Defender Check-In" inside the Seminole SAFE app, transmitting their exact room number and physical description to responding FSUPD tactical units to prevent friendly-fire misidentification.

B. Statutory High-Risk Exclusions: FSU retains full statutory authority under F.S. § 790.06 to prohibit concealed carry inside ticketed athletic venues (Doak Campbell Stadium, Dick Howser Stadium, and the Donald L. Tucker Civic Center), high-hazard magnetic or chemical laboratories (the National High Magnetic Field Laboratory and Dirac Science Library labs), formal academic or employment disciplinary hearings, and shared freshman residence halls (such as Salley, Landis, Azalea, and Magnolia Halls).
